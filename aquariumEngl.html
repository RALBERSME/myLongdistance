Japanese waters are home to an impressive diversity. In northeastern Hokkaido, you'll find salmon (Oncorhynchus) that return to freshwater every year with loud currents to spawn. The Pacific coast of Honshu, Shikoku, and Kyushu offers a rich variety of species: mackerel (Scomber japonicus) migrate past in large swarms, while tuna such as the Atlantic bluefin (Thunnus obesus) or the bluefin tuna (Thunnus albacares) hunt in the warm upper reaches of the Gulf Stream branches. Deeper zones around the Japanese archipelago are home to giant catfish, orange rockfish, and various swordfish, whose colorful patterns fascinate both fishing nets and underwater photographers.

Roundfish like the yellowfin tuna play a special role. It is found seasonally off the coast of Japan and is valued by many fishermen for its cultural and historical significance. The coasts around Okinawa boast an extraordinary biodiversity, including maiso, a deep-sea hermit crab, and a number of scorpionfish species, whose vibrant colors are particularly striking when viewed at night in the makowave light of the deep.

Smaller, but by no means less important, are the genus of sea cucumbers, which inhabit rocky coastal zones and are characterized by powerful dorsal fins. In Japan's river systems, especially in Hokkaido's inland waters, you can find trout species, including the brown trout, which is a seasonal highlight for many anglers.

For tourists, there is an exciting prospect: fishing guides report on good spots along verdant coastal sections, where the ebb and flow of the tides reveal new feeding grounds. Local fishermen tell of schools of fish resembling tongmen carpets and of the patience without which no catch is possible. The sea off Japan is not a static place; it changes with seasons, currents, and climate change. Yet it remains a source of food, fascination, and stories – from the simple, robust fillets in the morning to the nightly luminous episodes when anglers scan the surfaces with lamps for glimmers.

Japanese waters harbor not only marine life, but also maritime stories that extend beyond fish. One particularly captivating chapter revolves around ships, harbors, and the diverse coastal culture. In the low-tide spring, traditionally anchored nets glide through the currents, while modern cargo and passenger ships cruise between Tokyo Bay, Osaka, and Nagasaki harbors. The giants of the sea, such as LNG carriers or Ro-Ro ships, leave a silent but impressive silhouette in the night light – a contrast of technology and sea.

An intriguing example is the story of the “Umi no Shōkansha” – a local legend about an old fishing boat that has been considered a lucky charm for generations. On calm nights, it is said to glide silently through the harbor entrance as if it were part of the tides. Divers report seeing markings on the hull that supposedly point to ancient trade routes. Whether true or myth, the stories attract people: photographers and historians find inspiration in the traces left by water, salt, and time.

There are also fascinating underwater phenomena beyond the fish: Shipwrecks in coastal areas, which become artificial reefs, inhabited by mussels, shrimp, and tiny creatures. Divers can discover relics from bygone trading eras near Ishigaki or Shimoda, while gentle currents reveal steel plates and sail remnants.

The coastal culture itself offers vibrant experiences: stretches of coastline with traditional dockside restaurants where freshly caught seafood is served right on the quay, or Taiyo Marine experiments that combine sustainable fishing with tourism. Night markets at harbor entrances offer delicacies, stories, and local music that reflects the rhythm of the sea.
